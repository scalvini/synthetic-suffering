# Synthetic suffering database memo

> **Version:** 2.1  
> **Last updated:** 6 November 2026

This memo accompanies synthetic_suffering_cases.xlsx (sheets Cases and Codebook), synthetic_suffering_cases.csv and synthetic_suffering_changelog.md, which hold 113 cases from two rounds of searching; the annex lists every candidate that was not entered.

## 1. Assumptions

- **Two rounds.** The second round (28 October 2026) added 59 cases (C55 to C113) from 186 searches to the 54 cases and 198 searches of the first, above the target of 30 to 50, and it changed eight first-round rows, as the changelog records.
- **Searching.** Every genre has now been searched, but 112 of the 186 second-round searches were in English and none in Russian or Malay; the AI Incident Database, WITNESS, Bellingcat and Google Scholar were reached through searches naming them.
- **Seed cases.** Table 2 is entered as C01 to C13, with corrections in the Notes field; C10 is coded as health and C11 as activism, and C13 is kept although it was never published.
- **Verification.** A producer's statement quoted in reporting counts as a primary source for the status, although the report itself is tagged secondary; two reports count as independent only when neither republishes the other; and two important unverified cases are kept (C13, C32).
- **Coding.** C61 is coded as war reporting, the only news genre, C94 and C99 as victims' advocacy and a police union's image (C81) as activism; new codes, such as a bracketed qualifier of participation, are defined in the Codebook; four precedents from 2019 to 2021 are flagged (C55, C90 to C92); real images dismissed as AI are cited in field 22; and no image was reproduced or generated.
- **Wording.** On 6 November 2026 the records were reworded to name the image, the person depicted or the number meant in each place, and field 11 is now called "What the image depicts"; quotations and source titles are unchanged.

## 3. The thirteen cases most useful for the paper

1. **C02, Amnesty International Norway on Colombia (April 2023).** Labelled illustrations of police abuse, justified by the protesters' safety, drew warnings that they help governments dismiss real footage, so the case tests whether protection and a label confer evidentiary standing (compare C09).
2. **C03, Exhibit A-i (April 2023).** Images generated from detainees' statements were workshopped with the witnesses and disclosed as "not real but the experiences they depict are" (Exhibit A-i website, 2023), yet a survivor said that the consultation made her relive her trauma, which marks the limit of participation as remedy.
3. **C12, AI poverty stock images (October 2025), with C18.** Stock images of typified poverty, labelled as AI on Adobe Stock, still reproduce racial stereotypes, so harm survives disclosure and responsibility is spread across contributors, platforms and buyers (compare C18 on the Gaza war).
4. **C11, All Eyes on Rafah (May 2024), with C77.** Shared by tens of millions of accounts and chosen because platforms remove graphic photographs, the image was criticised for displacing the documentary record, the clearest case of sanitised solidarity, and a generated infant victim of 7 October answered it (C77).
5. **C34, Hurricane Helene (October 2024), with C69.** Told that the crying girl with a puppy was generated, Amy Kremer of the Republican National Committee kept it online and wrote "honestly, it doesn't matter" (X post quoted by NPR, October 2024), so its appellative force outlived its testimonial claim, as did Sora videos of Black women misusing food benefits that Fox News reported as real (C69).
6. **C20, the puppy edit of a burned infant photograph (October 2023), with C60.** An AI-edited copy of an official Israeli photograph circulated as the original to claim that the dead infant was fabricated, the most direct case of the liar's dividend, and images derived from generated flood videos were later offered as proof that suffering in Gaza is staged (C60).
7. **C61, ZDF heute journal (February 2026), with C57.** A public broadcaster aired a Sora clip of a mother taken from her crying children as footage of immigration raids, then withdrew the report, apologised and judged that a label would not have sufficed (compare the Libération front page in C57).
8. **C37, Holocaust victims on monetised Facebook pages (May 2025).** Pages turned names from Yad Vashem and photographs from the Auschwitz Memorial into generated portraits with invented lives, and Meta found no breach of its rules, which shows epistemic extraction from the archive of the dead.
9. **C47, American Murder: Gabby Petito (February 2025), with C41.** A synthetic voice reads the murdered woman's journals with her family's permission and a disclaimer, yet her mother said that it sounds slightly wrong, so harm survives consent and disclosure; Welcome to Chechnya (C41), whose synthetic faces cover consenting people who were filmed, is the contrast case.
10. **C97, Christopher Pelkey (May 2025).** A video scripted by his sister made the dead man forgive his killer at the sentencing, and the Arizona Court of Appeals vacated the sentence in September 2026 because the video "erases the interpretive distance" (FOX 10 Phoenix, October 2026) between her beliefs and his, the clearest ruling on evidentiary versus appellative use.
11. **C96, The Shotline (February 2024), with C95.** Bereaved parents and survivors sent the cloned voices of people killed by guns to members of Congress, and relatives of the disappeared in Jalisco script what animated photographs say (C95), so family authorship answers participation as remedy while the dead still voice words they never said.
12. **C107, Sedar Soares (May 2022), with C111.** Rotterdam police and the family made a deepfake of the murdered boy so that the appeal "harten kan raken in het criminele milieu" (would touch hearts in the criminal world, police statement quoted by NOS, May 2022), whereas a sheriff's ChatGPT image of an unidentified dead man was judged by whether relatives recognised him (C111).
13. **C109, State of Washington v. Puloka (February 2024), with C113.** A court excluded an AI-enhanced video of a fatal shooting because it "does not show with integrity what actually happened" (the court's order as quoted by Criminal Legal News, December 2025), while AI-sharpened stills of the killing of Alex Pretti circulated as real frames (C113).
